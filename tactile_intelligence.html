Tactile Intelligence

“To touch is to understand — robots that learn as they feel.”

Robots now see and plan well, yet they still fail at the moment of contact. Friction changes, objects slip, and the contact patch is hidden from cameras, so the quantities that matter most for manipulation are the ones a robot can measure least directly. Tactile intelligence treats touch as a closed loop: tactile signals update a belief about the hidden contact state, an uncertainty-aware controller acts on that belief, and the action itself creates the next tactile measurement.

Figure 1. The tactile intelligence loop. Contact produces tactile signals (1); a latent stochastic model turns them into a belief over the hidden contact state—slip, friction, object pose (2); a controller plans with that belief and its uncertainty (3); and the resulting grip or surface-haptic action changes the contact, which produces the next measurement (4).

Motivation

I want robots that can feel, adapt, and decide through touch. My work in robust, adaptive and Bayesian model predictive control, combined with reinforcement learning, suggests a shift in viewpoint: compliance need not be only a passive mechanical property; it can be an active principle of intelligence. A compliant robot should not resist uncertainty at contact, but yield to it, interpret it, and learn from it—while still meeting the safety and stability guarantees that control theory demands.

Problem formulation

Let \(z_t\) be the latent contact state (e.g., slip velocity, friction coefficient, object pose), \(u_t\) the grip or haptic command, and \(y_t\) the tactile measurement, which is noisy and only partially informative:

\[ z_{t+1}=f_\vartheta(z_t,u_t)+w_t,\qquad y_t=h_\psi(z_t)+\nu_t,\qquad b_t=p\bigl(z_t\mid y_{0:t},u_{0:t-1}\bigr). \]

The controller chooses actions from the belief \(b_t\), not from the true state:

\[ u_t=\pi(b_t)=\arg\min_{u_{t:t+N-1}}\ \mathbb{E}_{b_t}\!\Bigl[\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\ell(z_{t+k},u_{t+k})\Bigr] \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \Pr\bigl(\text{slip}_{t+k}\bigr)\le\epsilon . \]

The central difficulty is that \(f_\vartheta\) and \(h_\psi\) are learned and adapt online, so the belief that the controller trusts can itself change in ways that are not safe. Making that interface safe is the common thread of my current work.

Figure 2. How learning from touch is kept safe for the controller. Figure 1 shows what the loop does; this figure shows how its weakest link—the learned belief that the controller trusts—is certified. (a) Every correction proposed by the tactile encoder is projected within a budget \(\delta_E\) chosen from the input tolerance \(\bar u_c\), so no correction moves the grip or haptic command by more than \(\bar u_c\). (b) A learning update is accepted only if it lowers the prediction score and moves the belief by at most \(\varepsilon_B\), chosen from the update tolerance \(\bar u_a\). This two-budget structure is the one I certify for MPC in Neural Belief MPC; in wearable haptics the tolerances come from the user as well as the actuator.

Core ideas

Latent tactile state. Deep stochastic state-space models capture what touch cannot measure directly—slip, friction and contact geometry—together with their uncertainty and temporal structure.
Belief-aware control. MPC handles constraints such as force limits and slip margins; reinforcement learning supplies long-term adaptation. Both act on the belief, not on a single estimate.
Bidirectional touch. Surface haptics actively modulate fingertip friction, so the robot can both sense and shape contact—blurring the line between perception and action.

Research questions

  • How can a learned tactile model keep a calibrated belief when contact is intermittent and sensors saturate or drop out?
  • How far may an online-adapted belief move before the controller's grip command changes by more than a safe tolerance?
  • When should the controller trust touch, and when should it fall back to a conservative, model-based action?

Foundations in my current work

Each question builds on results I have developed for learned state estimation and control under partial observation:

  • Seeing Beyond Vision — bounded-error latent belief tracking when the sensor is occluded, the visual analogue of intermittent contact.
  • Robust CF Filtering and SafeCF-SSM — cognitive-flexible filtering and calibrated safety monitoring of decoded latent predictions under online adaptation.
  • Neural Belief MPC — certified belief budgets that limit how far a neural observer may move the input plan of an MPC.

Objective

To develop robots that yield to uncertainty yet remain stable, safe and dexterous—advancing tactile robotics toward truly human-compatible interaction in manipulation, assistance and rehabilitation.
